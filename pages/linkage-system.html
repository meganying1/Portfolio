Linkage System

Designing and iterating a motor-driven linkage that converts rotary motion into extended linear button actuation.

Overview

In a team of three, I helped develop a linkage system that converts continuous motor rotation into vertical motion for pressing a button. The goal was to maximize the amount of time the button remained depressed during a two-minute operating cycle while meeting geometric, structural, and motor-loading constraints.

We developed the mechanism through concept generation, kinematic analysis, stress calculations, FEA, fabrication, and physical testing. After the first prototype performed significantly below prediction, we redesigned the linkage geometry and increased button press time from 2.96 seconds to 41.17 seconds.

Concept Development

We began by exploring several linkage configurations inspired by four-bar and crank-rocker mechanisms. Our concepts varied the location of slotted joints and the lengths of the rocker, coupler, and crank to control the motion of the output link.

We focused on slotted designs because the relative motion between the pin and slot could delay the descent of the rocker and increase the time the mechanism remained near its maximum height. After comparing three concepts, we selected a single-slot design that provided the best balance between dwell time and structural loading.

The final architecture used a crank-rocker mechanism with a pin sliding through a slot in the output link. We also verified that the linkage satisfied the Grashof criterion so the motor-driven crank could rotate continuously.

Mechanical Design

The motor drives the shortest link as a crank. The crank transfers motion through the coupler to the slotted rocker, which drives the button-pressing assembly vertically.

The slot allows the coupler connection to move relative to the rocker rather than forcing a fixed rotational relationship. This was intended to keep the rocker near its upright position for a longer portion of each cycle, increasing the mechanism's dwell time at the button.

We selected the linkage lengths and mounting locations to maximize button press duration while keeping the assembly within the available workspace and avoiding excessive motor or structural loading.

Engineering Analysis

I calculated the forces and stresses in the mechanism at critical orientations and used these results to size the acrylic links. The linkage was fabricated from 1/4-inch acrylic, with link widths selected to meet the desired factor of safety while minimizing unnecessary material.

The team also used FEA to evaluate stress and deflection under the critical loading condition. For the initial design, the simulation predicted a maximum stress of approximately 288 psi at the maximum-height configuration.

We combined the structural analysis with motion studies to balance strength, weight, and button press time. The analysis informed changes to link dimensions and geometry before the final iteration.

Fabrication & Testing

We fabricated the linkage components from acrylic using DXF files generated from the CAD model, then assembled the mechanism onto the test fixture.

During the first test, the mechanism pressed the button for only 2.96 seconds compared with a theoretical prediction of 30.66 seconds. It completed the motion without structural failure or motor stall, but the rocker descended much faster than expected.

We traced the behavior to the linkage geometry, particularly the coupler length and the effect of downward loading on the rocker. Based on these observations, we increased the rocker and coupler lengths, shortened the crank, and changed the rocker mounting position before repeating the motion and structural analysis.

Results

The revised mechanism increased button press time from 2.96 seconds to 41.17 seconds during the two-minute test, an improvement of approximately 38 seconds. The rocker descended smoothly, and the system completed the test without structural failure or motor stall.
