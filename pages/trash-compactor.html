Trash Compactor

Designing and building a standalone trash compactor that automatically compresses household waste.

Overview

In a team of five, I helped develop a compact trash compactor from early concept development through engineering analysis, fabrication, assembly, and testing.

The final design uses a linear actuator to drive a scissor-lift mechanism that raises a compression plate through the bin. I developed the assembly CAD and designed the custom actuator mounts used to integrate the linkage into the surrounding structure.

The completed prototype was demonstrated at the Carnegie Mellon Mechanical Engineering Design Expo, where the project received the Best Overall Project Award.

Concept Development

We began by benchmarking existing trash compactors and identifying limitations in available products. Built-in models were expensive and required permanent installation, while lower-cost alternatives relied heavily on manual force. From this research, we defined the primary design requirements around compaction performance, packaging, ease of use, safety, manufacturability, and cost.

We generated several concepts and compared them using weighted design criteria. We selected a scissor-lift mechanism driven by a linear actuator because it fit within the available footprint while providing the mechanical advantage needed for compression.

Throughout concept development, we also considered DFMA, FMEA, and DFE principles to identify manufacturing, assembly, reliability, and environmental considerations before fabrication.

Mechanical Design

The compaction mechanism sits beneath the trash compartment. A linear actuator drives a scissor linkage that raises a compression plate vertically through the bin. During operation, the plate compresses the waste against the underside of the lid, holds the load for approximately ten seconds, then retracts automatically.

One of the main design challenges was integrating the actuator with the linkage. The actuator had a fixed hole pattern and limited mounting locations, so the interface had to accommodate those constraints while positioning the actuator correctly and transferring load into the mechanism.

I developed the assembly CAD in SolidWorks and designed custom actuator mounts around the existing hole locations. The mounts secured the actuator within the available packaging space and created the interface between the actuator and linkage.

Engineering Analysis

We analyzed the linkage before fabrication to determine whether the selected actuator could generate sufficient compaction force.

Using free-body diagrams and static force analysis, we evaluated how actuator force propagated through the scissor mechanism under different loading conditions. The analysis predicted that the mechanism could support approximately 6.4 to 12.8 lb before the actuator stalled, depending on the distribution of reaction forces.

We also evaluated the structural capacity of the aluminum linkage using factor-of-safety calculations. The results showed that the linkage could withstand loads well above the actuator's output, indicating that actuator capacity would limit performance before structural failure occurred. Physical testing later confirmed this prediction.

Fabrication & Testing

The team shared responsibility for milling, turning, 3D printing, assembly, and testing. The linkage was fabricated from aluminum bars and rods using manual machining, while the custom actuator mounts were 3D printed. We assembled the linkage, actuator, compression plate, enclosure, electronics, hinges, and lid-locking hardware into a complete working prototype.

Testing showed that the linkage remained structurally intact while the actuator reached its force limit under heavier loads. This matched the analytical results and identified a higher-capacity actuator as the clearest improvement for a future iteration.

Manufacturing Considerations

Although the prototype used machining, 3D printing, and a wooden enclosure for rapid fabrication, we also planned for higher-volume production. I contributed DFM input by selecting manufacturing processes for each component and refining part geometry to support those methods and simplify assembly.

Results

The final prototype successfully completed an automated compaction cycle within a standalone trash-bin form factor. Testing confirmed that actuator capacity, rather than linkage strength, limited the achievable compression force.

The project received the Best Overall Project Award at the Carnegie Mellon Mechanical Engineering Design Expo.
