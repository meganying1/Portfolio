Battery Door

Designing a one-touch battery door mechanism that releases with a downward button press and automatically re-latches when closed.

Overview

For an Apple mechanical design interview, I developed a battery door mechanism that opens with a button integrated within the door perimeter. I began by defining the mechanical and packaging requirements, then explored multiple release architectures before selecting and refining a spring-loaded sliding latch.

I developed the final design in Siemens NX and sized the mechanism around the required latch travel and button motion. I then evaluated the design through mechanical calculations, tolerance analysis, FEA, material selection, and manufacturing planning. The final nine-component mechanism provides one-touch release and automatically re-latches when the door is closed.

Concept Development

I began by defining the design requirements and assumptions, including packaging constraints, button force and travel, opening and closing forces, durability, cost, and production volume.

I then developed four release concepts based on moving snap fits, push-release latches, rotating cam mechanisms, and sliding latches. I evaluated each through its complete release and reset sequence, comparing mechanical complexity, tolerance sensitivity, accidental actuation risk, packaging, and user interaction.

A moving snap fit provided the simplest and most tolerance-robust architecture, so I developed two implementations around it. The first used angled slots to translate vertical button travel into horizontal latch motion. The second rotated the latch out of engagement.

After modeling both in Siemens NX, I compared them using a weighted decision matrix. The rotating design offered lower cost and a smaller package, while the sliding design performed better in the highest-weighted criteria of effectiveness and user experience. I selected the sliding architecture for detailed development.

Mechanical Design

The release mechanism consists of a vertically translating button, horizontally translating latch, return springs, and top and bottom chassis.

Pegs on the button engage angled slots in the latch. Pressing the button drives the pegs downward through these slots, translating the latch away from the door until it clears the door's snap-fit feature. Spring-loaded hinges then lift the released door.

Releasing the button allows the springs to return both components to their latched positions. During closing, a ramp on the door's snap fit drives the latch backward until the feature passes beneath it. The springs then push the latch forward to re-engage automatically. A flat locking surface prevents upward force on the closed door from retracting the latch.

I designed the top chassis to snap onto the bottom chassis, capturing the internal components while constraining the button and latch to their intended directions of motion.

Engineering Analysis

I sized the mechanism by working backward from the latch travel required for full door release. This determined the button travel, angled-slot geometry, and spring requirements.

I then analyzed the components most susceptible to repeated loading over the 1,000-cycle durability requirement. Hand calculations showed that stresses in the button pegs and door snap fit remained below the relevant material limits, while snap-fit strain stayed within the allowable range. I also calculated the snap-fit deflection force to verify that the required door-closing force remained within the design requirement.

Because latch engagement depends on the accumulated dimensions of several components, I performed a worst-case tolerance stack-up across the mechanism. The analysis confirmed that sufficient latch overlap remained at the dimensional extremes.

Finally, I used FEA to evaluate the two primary loading events: contact between the button pegs and sliding latch during release, and snap-fit deflection as the door closes. I compared stress, strain, and deformation against the hand calculations and material limits to verify the final geometry.

Manufacturing & Cost

I selected materials and manufacturing processes based on geometry, mechanical requirements, production volume, and surface requirements.

The chassis, latch, and structural portion of the door use injection-molded ABS, providing both the compliance required by the snap-fit features and compatibility with high-volume production. The exposed button and door cover use aluminum for lightweight, durable user-contact surfaces. Springs, screws, and spring-loaded hinges are purchased components rather than custom parts.

Based on the proposed materials, processes, and purchased hardware, I estimated a production cost of approximately $3.30 per assembly.

Results

The final design uses nine components to provide one-touch release and automatic re-latching within the required package. Mechanical calculations, tolerance analysis, and FEA verified operation across dimensional variation and repeated loading, while the proposed material and manufacturing strategy resulted in an estimated unit cost of $3.30.
